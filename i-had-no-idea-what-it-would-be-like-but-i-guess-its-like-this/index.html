I Had No Idea What It Would Be Like, but I Guess It’s Like This

Anna Brežģis · November 2026

B is walking back to the annex from the job talk. I feel like we should walk back together, because I am also going back to my desk in the annex. It could be nice, a conversation between us.

I jog-step a few paces ahead so I catch up with him. “Can I ask you a mathematics question?” I ask.

B has a PhD in pure math. I know enough to talk at a level of high abstraction, and I can understand what follows from an implication without understanding the mechanics behind it. I’ve grown familiar with this way of talking about machine learning. Oh yes, the loss surface. The kernel alignment. A learned bilinear map.

I am competent yet disfluent. The talk today had one of those words in it that I never bothered to learn properly. Perhaps B can tell me.

“Yeah, sure,” he said. And suddenly we are walking together. We turn down the hallway, and we go across the glass footbridge between the two buildings. Sunlight is coming in from both directions. It’s a few degrees warmer there.

“What does affine mean?”

I watch another group of colleagues pass and I feel my question carry. If they don’t know this word, then I am casually brilliant. If they do, I am the most underqualified person to ever walk the halls of this laboratory.

“It means that you add a constant to it.” He begins. And his tone doesn’t seem to suggest that I am stupid or incompetent or ridiculous. It’s friendly, and a little curious. It’s almost like I asked him something that he wanted to answer. “A linear transformation requires that the zero vector maps to zero. In an affine transformation…”

I’m getting my blood drawn at the doctor’s office because I’m worried that there’s something horribly wrong with me. I asked them to test a bunch of things. Perhaps I’m pregnant or dying.

I learn later that there’s nothing wrong, in the physiological sense. I have just been overwhelmed with stress since my husband has left to Boston for his internship. That’s most likely why I am exhausted, dizzy, and can’t sleep through the night.

“I’m really afraid of needles,” I warn the physician. She’s looking at me with big, concerned eyes, and I feel the need to elaborate. I can see her visualizing me coming to on the crinkly-papered table, tactfully informing me that I passed out during a routine medical procedure. I continue, “I’m not going to like, pass out or anything. I just kind of freak out a little bit. Can you distract me during, like talk a lot?”

The other attendee is a nurse practitioner in training. I learned this while we made small talk, waiting for the blood pressure cuff to fill and release.

“Would you like me to hold your hand?” The nurse-practitioner-in-training asks.

I feel the brief need to recoil, to laugh, to quickly reassure her beyond any possible doubt that I would not like that, not at all. But I am trying to be polite, and in the moment that I hesitate to respond, the need passes. What’s left in its place is hungry, absurd relief.

“Uh, yeah, actually.” I try to display a normal amount of acceptance. “Can you do that?”

They use a butterfly needle in the fat blue vein on the back of my hand. The ones at my elbows are tiny and finnicky. While we wait for the little vials to fill, the student nurse’s palm goes warm in mine. And we make a little more small talk to distract me from becoming too aware of my physical circumstance and passing out indeed.

❦

I’m not that afraid of needles, as it turns out, or I’ve grown out of it in my twenties. Because a little over a year later, I’m laid out on the acupuncturist’s table while a serene flute accompaniment plays overhead. I was having a sciatica flare-up and this was my latest grab at short-term relief.

The very first time, I was absolutely terrified. I asked for the same thing, distraction during the insertion, but then she left me alone for half an hour for the needles to work on me. Psychically, I do everything in my power to ignore the fact that I’m covered, head to toe, with needles 3mm deep in my epidermis.

I made the mistake of mentioning TMJ, and she lines my jawline with little pricks, and one between my eyebrows where my third eye would be. I feel like Pinhead from Hellraiser. Or a sea urchin. There’s this little tug-of-war going on in my brain, almost like waves washing up on a beach and receding.

My inner monologue sounds something like this: Okay, think about something else. Something nice. What’s nice? Perhaps let’s think of sunshine. Remember that one time where—THERE ARE NEEDLES IN YOU—No, no. Stop that, not right now. I can not freak out right now. Okay, calm. Calmness. Breathe in—NEEDLE—no. Breathe in… breathe out. Good, good. This is actually going okay. Oh yeah, I have needles—NO. What’s the litany against fear, let’s think of that. NEEDLE. No—Litany against fear. I must not fear, for fear is the little death, fear is… YOU ARE GOING TO DIE FROM NEEDLES—no. Stop. I will allow my fear to pass through me…

I briefly entertain absurd thoughts. Perhaps I’ve actually died and this is hell. It makes sense that hell would exercise peoples’ phobias. Maybe this is what I get for never properly believing in God. What if I’m dreaming and I wake up at home in bed? What if I’m alive, but I will die from acupuncture, is that possible?

She comes back in thirty minutes as promised and takes them out, one by one. The little pull is weird, and makes me cringe. But the relief is humongous. I had never felt so glad in my entire life to see another human being. I had to consciously stop myself from saying Oh, you’re BACK when the door opened.

She asks me to turn over, so she can work on my back. I realize, as I lift myself up from the table and shift, that I am completely, entirely relaxed. My body feels heavy and slow. I settle down on the paper and breathe out.

“How do you feel?” she asks. I wonder if she could hear my deliberate mindful breathing earlier.

“Have you ever smoked marijuana?” I ask. “I mean, I don’t really smoke anything, but the times that I have… that’s kind of how this feels.”

She agrees that yes, that she can see how it feels like that. She did not confirm or deny whether she has ever smoked marijuana.

The next times it’s not so bad. Or it’s less and less bad every time. I don’t think it actually did anything for my sciatica, but I liked feeling like I had just smoked marijuana.

❦

“Heyyyy, Anna.”

B again. He has this excellent habit of greeting everyone by name the first time he sees them for the first time every day. Every single time, it’s this little flicker of warm recognition.

I wonder if that’s the intended effect, or if he learned it from that book that everyone knows. The sweetest sound in the world… But he doesn’t use it too much like an insurance broker. It’s just enough, and he’s not trying to sell me something.

Perhaps it’s because it’s my name, or if it’s the particular pattern of sounds, the rising-and-falling of vowel to vowel. Like a birdcall, like the tone of voice you use to gentle a horse.

❦

There is a bug on the floor during the lab meeting. It was probably a seed bug, with long, articulated legs, and antennas feeling around the floor. However it got here, it was a long way from home in the basement of the physics wing.

After the group had noticed the bug, Y had stepped on it. She is praised for her quick and decisive action, and the group goes back to discussing the current project.

After a moment, I notice its little legs reaching out again, squirming on the floor. Eventually, it seems to have somewhat un-squished itself, dragging itself across the floor, a couple legs non-functional. It moves heaving step by heaving step. I can’t look away, even though I don’t really like looking at stepped-on bugs.

I lean sideways towards Y. “It’s still moving, you have to step on it again.”

She replies, “It’s fine, it’ll die anyway.” The bug has by now managed to cross half of a single tile.

It seems like it is regaining strength and resolve. I feel like I am watching an epic drama. I wonder if it’s in horrible pain, or if it cares. “How can you just let him suffer?” I ask.

At this point, the others have stopped talking. I look up from the bug to them. I have the familiar, sinking feeling of a classroom gone silent due to a single disruptive student.

I wave my hands frantically, as if trying to swipe the interaction from the environment. “Nope, done! That’s it. We’re done talking.”

The meeting continues, and Y and I watch the bug that she refused to kill a second time. It crosses tile after tile, walking halfway from the site of the botched squishing to almost the back of the room, then doubling back, creeping dangerously close to another lab member’s shoe.

We don’t say anything to him. We just scoot our chairs back to give the bug a wide berth. She leans in and we talk in whispers about the bug. “It’s so close,” she says. “It went so far,” I say.

❦

None of us speak Vietnamese. And none of them speak Russian or English, but because of the advances of automatic translation technology and our egoless gesturing, we are able to buy fruit.

When you ask about fruit, they give you a piece. The seller has big, calloused hands with square fingers. He peels away the rind or the seeds for you, of the alien fruit.

It’s mealy and tannic. There are round black seeds that get in the way of the meat, almost like little pebbles. I’m eating around them rather than taking them all in my mouth, because I don’t want to spit out seeds in public. I’m not sure that my approach is better, but it’s mine.

The seller notices and takes the fruit gently away from my mouth, where I’m holding it with both hands. He peels back the rind slowly, demonstrating. Without any language, saying here, this is how. And I don’t try myself, I just take the meat he’s prepared, and he peels more.

The whole world has shrunk to the humid shade of the tent, two pairs of hands, and the fruit between us. Everything is sticky and dry. Rid of all my phatic customs, with none of my clever language, I can only take and eat. And so I do, again and again, until this one is gone.

We hobble back to the villa heavy with fruit. In our hands are bags and bags of produce: mango, bananas, watermelon, something foreign to us. We have a few coconuts with the tops sliced off so we can drink their juice. None of the berry, which I didn’t actually like and would prefer not to have again.

❦

The other lady is getting acrylic nails done. She seems concerned about the color she’s chosen. Apricot could potentially come out as traffic-cone orange. The tech doing my nails assures her that it’s orange with a little pink, a lovely color.

She keeps making leading, gentle attempts at conversation. I recognize them as the classics. Are you adjusting to the time change? Have you done anything nice this weekend? Do you have plans for Thanksgiving?

About the time change, the tech working on my nails replies, “Thank God for Halloween, Thanksgiving, and Christmas, because otherwise I would leave this place!”

I wonder if she’s lonely, and this nail salon is her only opportunity for social interaction and focused personal attention. Someone’s warm hands on yours. Being a treasured guest. The staff, clearly a mom and pop duo, answer her attempts but fall back into silence eventually. Perhaps it’s because Titanic is on the TV, and we’re all half-watching it.

I have never properly watched Titanic. I never thought to, probably because of the ambient social buzz about the movie. People say that Rose was stupid, that she could have taken the lifeboat, and on the floating door, there must have been room for two.

But when I watch it now, I see something compellingly, achingly human about Rose. How she scrambles back up from the lifeboat to get back to Jack. The other passengers over the railing and onto the lower deck, and she makes a mad dash across the ship to get to him.

I can almost feel it as well. How you’d do absolutely anything for one more moment, anything at all. You’d condemn yourself, maim yourself, destroy everything you have and are, just to have one second longer. At the moment of reunion there’d be rushing, total relief. There would be neither before nor later, just a beating heart of now, now, now.

I think about that too, all the bargains I’ve made with God. I’d do absolutely everything right. I’d be good, my best behavior. I’d go to church, eat my vegetables, brush my teeth morning and night. I’d never, ever do anything wrong ever again. Just please, please, please. And God doesn’t answer, he never does, because my currency doesn’t spend in this life. Like credit-cards at a stick-up. I pull out my pockets and shake out my bags to find nothing useful at all.

When I turn back to the table, the tech is lifting up my hands, folding the towel to a fresh side. I rest my wrists on the low black cushion, and I realize that my hands are trembling faintly. I feel Rose’s dumb, bottomless wanting all through my veins. I’m shaking with the effort to not feel it.

Behind the tech, there’s a mirror on the wall. It’s there I suppose to make the salon look bigger, to catch the light that comes in from the street-facing windows. I look myself in the eyes. I don’t look devastated by grief, or consumed by unsatisfied animal desire. I look plain, and tired, and rumpled.

The tech asks me if I want my nails just filed down. I look down at my hands. I consider saying yes, thinking perhaps I’ll let them grow out. But my index finger’s nail is longer than the rest, and crooked where I had been biting it.

“Can you cut them actually? And this one here, I think it’s too long, can you make them all short?”
